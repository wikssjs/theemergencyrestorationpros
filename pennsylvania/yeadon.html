What insurance covers — and the exclusion that surprises people

The detail that costs Yeadon homeowners the most is this: standard insurance covers water damage from a sudden internal event, like a burst washing machine hose, but not surface flooding — water that comes in from outside, whether from a flash storm or rising rivers, is excluded unless you have separate flood insurance. Yeadon’s proximity to the Delaware River, and older drainage infrastructure, mean that some unlucky residents learn about this gap the hard way.

Insurance also has limits for gradual leaks and maintenance issues. If your claim stems from a pipe that’s been dripping inside a wall for months — common in 1920s–1950s homes here — coverage is likely denied. Sewer backup coverage is not standard but offered as an extra rider, well worth considering in older homes with cast-iron lines and in neighborhoods served by systems prone to overflow.

When you file, expect payment based on either “replacement cost” (new-for-old) or “actual cash value” (depreciated). Your contractor should provide a scope of loss: a clear, line-item estimate matching insurance requirements. Restoration firms often offer direct billing to your insurer, minimizing money up front. With “assignment of benefits,” you sign over part of the claim to the contractor, who deals with adjusters directly — but you lose some control, and it’s not always in your best interest on larger jobs.

In Pennsylvania, the Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act (HICPA) requires a written contract for jobs over $500 and gives you a three-business-day right to walk away. If any contractor offers to “waive your deductible,” that’s an illegal practice here and a red flag for insurance fraud. Choose firms that put everything in writing and comply with all consumer protections.

What can be saved and what has to go

Not everything wet after a leak in Yeadon can — or should — be saved. Older homes here, built 1920–1950, often have plaster instead of drywall, full basements, and cast-iron waste lines. When those pipes fail, water collects under floors or fills cavities behind vintage walls where moisture lingers for days.

  • Fiberglass insulation and carpet pad: Once soaked, these nearly always need disposal, especially after a sewer backup. Drying them in place is not recommended with the risk of invisible mold growth.
  • Drywall: If the water traveled less than 12 inches up the wall and was clean Category 1, sometimes patching is possible. But with gray or black water, especially from a failed cast-iron drain, drywall below the waterline should be cut and replaced.
  • Solid hardwood flooring: Can sometimes be saved if specialty drying mats are deployed within hours, but in Yeadon’s humid climate, delays usually mean warping. Engineered flooring and laminates almost always delaminate and need full replacement.
  • Plaster walls: Retain moisture long after the surface appears dry, making proper monitoring vital. Skipping demolition may seem to save money but often leads to late mold discoveries behind sound-looking walls.
  • Cabinets: Plywood boxes occasionally dry out if water is superficial, but particle board swells and loses integrity fast, especially where leaks settle under kitchen or bathroom bases — common in our houses with full basements.

If a contractor insists on gutting the whole area without clear evidence, push back — especially if the water was recent and not from a sewage event. But anyone who promises to “save everything” after water has been sitting in an older basement is setting you up for a mold claim down the road.

The second phase, reconstruction, is slower. After mitigation, there’s often a gap of several days to weeks before reconstruction starts. This is the moment when you might feel abandoned: the drying fans are gone, your basement is empty, and it seems nothing is happening. Strong communication with your contractor — especially about timelines and hand-offs — is the only way to avoid unwelcome surprises.

When storm remnants or a groundwater surge arrive, hydrostatic pressure can force water through older block walls, especially with the Delaware County region’s moderate freeze-thaw cycle and 30-inch frost depth stressing the foundation.

The single most common culprit for basement flooding here is sump pump failure, often after a power outage. If your basement is finished (drywall, insulation, laminate floors), every square foot impacted can double the scope of restoration compared to unfinished space because much more must be removed and replaced for code compliance and to pass humidity and air quality checks.

In older Yeadon homes, a burst cast-iron drain or sewer backup can mean loss of flooring, walls, and contents stored directly on the slab, putting your claim near the higher end of our local price ranges. Regulation, certification and verification in Yeadon

You should know: Pennsylvania does not license water restoration or mold remediation contractors. There’s no official trade license for this work — just a state-level Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration, required for contracts over $500 and easily checked. For mold and water mitigation, the real credential is IICRC certification: a national standard that insurers expect. Do not hesitate to ask the team lead for their IICRC card. The code here — Pennsylvania Uniform Construction Code (UCC), based on the IRC — matters most for reconstruction, not emergency drying or mitigation work.

Permits are rarely required for mitigation (tear-out and drying), unless you’re in a municipality like Upper Darby where demo involving drywall removal after water damage prompts a building permit. In most cases, basic drying in neighboring townships is permit-free, especially for minor leaks or wet-carpet jobs, such as in Springfield Township.

All employers are required to carry workers’ compensation, which you can verify via WCAIS. Before extraction begins, expect in writing: the HIC registration number, confirmation of insurance and comp coverage, the right to cancel, and an outline of the work. For homes built before 1978, ask if they follow the EPA Lead RRP Rule — and check their firm’s status at the federal site if you’re concerned about lead paint in demo areas.
