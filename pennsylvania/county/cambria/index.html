In addition, 78.2% of homes across the county were built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024), increasing the chances that even a small water loss can uncover pre-existing issues that require skilled containment or repair.

What can be saved and what has to go

In Cambria County—especially in Johnstown and Westmont where most homes were built before 1940—you’re likely dealing with full basements, traditional plaster walls, and a lot of old plumbing. When water damage happens, what can be salvaged depends entirely on the materials and how long they’ve been wet.

Fiberglass insulation and carpet pad almost always need to be removed. Once saturated, these materials trap moisture and become a breeding ground for mold—no reputable contractor will try to dry them in place. Drywall below the waterline (typically 12–24 inches up) is usually cut out; it wicks water fast, and behind it you’ll often find soaked insulation or framing. Plaster, which is common in Cambria County’s older homes, holds onto moisture much longer than drywall and may require extended drying, but can sometimes be saved if the exposure was brief and there’s good airflow.

Solid hardwood flooring can occasionally be restored using specialty floor drying systems, but that’s only realistic if the water is removed fast and the boards aren’t cupping or buckling severely. Engineered wood rarely fares as well—its thin veneer delaminates quickly. Cabinets are a case-by-case call: particleboard bases almost always fail after direct water contact, while plywood box cabinets may survive if dried swiftly.

If a contractor insists on gutting everything down to the studs without clear evidence (testing meters in-hand, visible mold, structural damage), consider a second opinion. On the other hand, a promise to “save everything” after a multi-day flood is a red flag—hidden moisture in plaster, base trim, or subfloors almost guarantees future mold or odor claims if not properly addressed.

Category and class: the two words that set your price

Homeowners often hear “category” and “class” used during a water loss but understandably blur the two. In restoration, they’re separate axes. “Category” means the cleanliness of the water: Category 1 is clean (supply line burst), Category 2 is gray (washing machine, sump backup), and Category 3 is black (“black water”—sewage, river, or water left standing over 72 hours). “Class,” by contrast, measures how much of the structure has absorbed water. Class 1 might be a corner of a room; Class 4 means deep penetration into plaster, hardwood, or masonry.

The price driver is not just how much area was wet, but how contaminated the water was. A small Category 3 loss (say, a couple of feet of sewer backup in a Johnstown basement) can run upward of $2,400–$11,500, which is more than some larger clean-water jobs. Category isn’t fixed at the moment of the leak: every hour water sits, it “ages.” Clean supply water left to pool in a finished basement over the weekend may degrade from Category 1 to Category 2, and over a couple days to hazardous Category 3, especially in humid conditions—turning a sub-$3,700 job into a full gut with biohazard protocols.

Category 3 cleanup in Cambria County

Sewage backups and floodwater cleanup (Category 3) aren’t just “worse water damage”—they’re a full biohazard requiring special handling. Every surface the water has touched is considered contaminated, so the response is not about odor or visible dirt. All crews here use PPE—full respirators, gloves, Tyvek suits—and set up containment zones. Porous materials like carpet, pad, insulation, and even lower drywall sections are cut out and discarded, no matter how clean they look. Disinfection is done using strong antimicrobial agents, with deodorization following.

Johnstown’s combined sewer overflow problem means these events aren’t outliers—they’re a standing municipal challenge. Basement backups often happen during storms when the stormwater and sewage pipes are overwhelmed. Prevention measures like backflow valves can help. Cambria County municipalities do not typically cost-share these, so homeowners usually bear installation costs unless a local grant is active.

Because the work involves everything from hazardous waste disposal to deodorizing the air, a typical Category 3 response runs $2,400–$11,500 depending on area and time exposed. Occupants should always be out during remediation—airborne bacteria and viruses are a real risk, not just a regulatory checkbox.

What a Pennsylvania restoration contractor must actually hold

Pennsylvania does not license restoration contractors or mold remediation firms at the state level—if someone in Cambria County hands you a trade “license” for these services, it’s a business registration, not a technical qualification. What matters is IICRC certification; ask to see the lead technician’s current IICRC card for S500 (water) or S520 (mold) work. For projects over $500, by law, the contract must be in writing and includes a three-business-day right to cancel.

Permits in Johnstown are typically required only if the work involves structural repairs—like replacing framing, subfloor, or making electrical alterations. Drying and in-place cleanup do not usually need a permit. Before work starts, ask the company for their Pennsylvania Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration number (all jobs over $500 require it), and for proof of workers’ compensation insurance if they employ anyone—this is verifiable online via WCAIS. General liability coverage is a must for any reputable contractor, though not a state mandate.

Always get a written scope of work and contract before extraction or demolition begins. Do not sign anything that is simply an estimate; the law requires a full contract with disclosures and a right of cancellation for your protection.

What to do right now

If you haven’t called for help yet, here’s what to do—step by step. First, shut off the main water supply to your home if the source is internal (burst pipe, leaking appliance). If water is pooling and you can safely reach your electrical panel (dry area, no flooded floor), turn off breakers to all affected rooms. Do not touch anything electrical if there’s any standing water near the panel.

Photograph everything: damage, standing water, affected furniture, baseboards—this is critical for both your insurer and the restoration company. Move what you safely can up off wet flooring—especially electronics and valuables. Don’t use a regular household vacuum to pick up standing water; it’s both dangerous and ineffective.

Call your insurance company after or at the same time as the restoration company—not after they’ve started destroying material. The order is less important than getting the documentation: make sure whoever answers can log your claim and advise you on next steps. Most policies require prompt action to limit damage—waiting until morning is where people lose out. If clean water sits overnight in a typical Johnstown finished basement, what might have been a $3,700 job can turn into a teardown and mold claim costing over $10,000 just because the water degraded while waiting.

Finished basements and water damage in Cambria County

Full basements are the default across Johnstown and its neighboring boroughs, thanks to hilly terrain and old-school masonry styles. That means when water strikes, it almost always finds its way below grade. Finished basements bring special problems: hydrostatic pressure from saturated ground can force water through foundation walls, drains may have nowhere to go when overloaded, and it’s common for standing water to get trapped behind drywall or paneling where it can’t be seen until demolition starts.

Sump pump failure is the number one culprit for finished basement loss in Cambria County homes. Installing a battery backup is a smart move—our frost depth of 36 inches and heavy snowmelt can knock out power just when pumps are needed most. In a finished space, what might be a minor cleanup in an unfinished basement quickly doubles in scope. Drywall, insulation, trim, carpet, and electronics usually all need special handling. Mold growth also becomes a larger risk, as hidden cavities make timely drying tricky.

Restoring a finished basement after water is simply a bigger project than drying out bare concrete—expect more demolition, longer drying times, and higher costs accordingly.

What insurance covers — and the exclusion that surprises people

The most expensive insurance misunderstanding in Cambria County is the difference between “water damage” and “flood.” If a supply line bursts, or your hot water tank lets go, that’s usually covered under your standard homeowners policy as a sudden accidental loss. But if water comes from outside—surface runoff, river overflow, or groundwater during a CSO event—it’s usually excluded. For that, you need a separate flood policy, typically through the National Flood Insurance Program. Many homeowners in Johnstown only learn this after the Stonycreek or Little Conemaugh River rises up.

Gradual leaks (like a small pinhole in old pipes) are a gray area—policies often exclude damage that is long-term or maintenance-related. Mold that results from an uncovered or neglected leak won’t be covered, but mold after a covered loss generally is, up to the policy’s limit. Sewer backup coverage must be added as a rider, not assumed.

Replacement cost coverage pays for new materials, while actual cash value factors in depreciation—so ask which you have upfront. Your restoration contractor will provide a scope of loss document, outlining exactly what needs doing; this is the basis for settlement.

In Pennsylvania, the Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act requires a written contract over $500, with a three-day cancellation right. Insurance deductible “waivers” are prohibited here—if a contractor offers to eat your deductible, it’s a red flag and a legal violation. Assignment of benefits allows you to sign over your claim to the contractor for direct billing, but you lose a measure of control over the process. Know what you’re agreeing to before you sign.

Frequently asked questions — water damage restoration in Cambria County

How does north-central Pennsylvania's climate affect drying times? The long, cold heating season in north-central Pennsylvania slows drying significantly compared to other parts of the state. In Cambria County, with an average of 6,300 heating degree days, relative indoor humidity becomes very low in winter when the furnace is on, but drying concrete, plaster, or hardwood can still take longer because of cooler temperatures and material thickness. Conversely, spring and summer bring heavy rain and higher humidity that make dehumidification more challenging. Expect water extraction and course drying to take 3–5 days for a typical job in Johnstown or Westmont, but work can sometimes stretch longer for deep-structure jobs—especially those in basements or older homes with thick masonry walls. This extra drying time can move costs upward, sometimes from the lower end up to $7,200 for larger or slower-drying losses.
Why is water damage priced per square foot? Restoration pricing in Cambria County is usually based on square footage because the amount of wet area directly affects equipment needs, labor hours, and drying time. The more walls, floors, and materials that are saturated, the more air movers, dehumidifiers, and demolition will be required. Pricing also reflects the materials (plaster, hardwood, carpet) common here, each needing specific handling. For example, the typical water extraction and drying project in Cambria County homes runs $1,150–$7,200 for a 1,000–2,000 sq ft affected area. A smaller kitchen leak may cost far less, while a full finished basement flood can land at the higher end of the band due to doubled square footage and complex demolition.
Can I rent dehumidifiers instead of hiring a company? You can rent a dehumidifier at a home improvement store, but most consumer models available in Cambria County are designed for damp basements, not for emergency drying after a loss. Commercial low grain refrigerant (LGR) dehumidifiers and high-velocity air movers used by restoration teams pull vastly more water out of building material and air per day. Without professional moisture meters and containment, DIY drying often leaves hidden pockets of moisture, especially behind plaster or in basements—leading to mold, odor, and eventual much higher costs. For reference, the price of professional water extraction and drying for a typical loss in the county starts at $1,150 but includes monitoring and warranty. If the loss is very minor and all surfaces are readily accessible and non-porous, rental may be reasonable—but it is usually not a safe option for older, finished interiors.
How do I verify a restoration contractor's insurance? In Pennsylvania, restoration firms must carry workers’ compensation insurance by law and should have general liability coverage. For Cambria County jobs, ask the contractor to provide a certificate of insurance showing your home’s address as an additional insured—then verify workers' compensation coverage using the state’s WCAIS system (not just a paper copy). Make sure any contractor supplying labor or demolition on site is listed on their certificate, not just the owner’s name. Never accept a verbal assurance or only a business card. If you’re hiring for a project over $950 (common for almost every restoration job here), this step protects you from legal and financial complications if a worker is injured or if there is property damage during the job.
Does insurance cover mold after water damage? In many cases, if the water loss itself is a “covered peril”—such as a burst pipe or hot water heater failure—your insurance will also cover mold remediation that results, subject to policy limits. However, insurers in Cambria County (and throughout Pennsylvania) often exclude mold if the water came from an uncovered source (like outside flooding, which is excluded) or if the moisture event was neglected and turned into a long-term maintenance issue. Mold remediation with containment for a home here typically costs $1,450–$10,000, so you should review your policy’s mold or fungi sub-limits. Keep all documentation for your claim, as adjusters often request proof that you responded quickly to the original water loss.
Do I need separate flood insurance in Cambria County? Yes—standard homeowners insurance does not cover groundwater, river flooding, or storm runoff events. In Cambria County, with the Stonycreek and Little Conemaugh rivers prone to flood, especially in Johnstown, separate flood coverage through the National Flood Insurance Program is necessary if you want to protect against these losses. Without it, the costs of water extraction, demolition, and repairs—often $3,700–$11,500 for large flood events—are entirely out of pocket. If you live in a designated floodplain or have ever seen water encroach your property during heavy rain, you should consider a separate policy.
Why does plaster take longer to dry than drywall? Plaster, which is installed throughout many pre-1940 Johnstown and Westmont homes, is much denser and thicker than modern drywall. Water tends to soak deeper into its layers and can wick up masonry behind, slowing the drying process. In Cambria County’s climate, this means air movers and dehumidifiers may run several extra days—even extending a project from the typical $2,900 drying to one at the upper end ($6,700) if deep plaster saturation is involved. Restoration companies must monitor the material’s moisture using meters, because dry-to-touch doesn’t equal truly dry, and cutting corners risks hidden mold and future damage.
What is a clearance test and do I need one? A clearance test is an air or surface sample taken after mold remediation is finished, to verify that the affected area in your Cambria County home meets acceptable health standards. It’s especially recommended in cases where visible mold was present, or where the work was covered by insurance and needs to satisfy a claim. For a typical mold remediation project here—costing $1,450–$10,000—the clearance test is usually done by a third party, ideally not the contractor who performed the work. While not required by law in Pennsylvania, most reputable firms and insurance adjusters expect it, particularly in older homes where hidden cavities and the risk of recurring mold are high.
Why did the crew cut my drywall two feet up? Cutting drywall two feet (24 inches) up is a standard practice in Cambria County after significant water damage. Drywall acts like a wick, pulling water vertically well above the visible water line, especially if it has been sitting for more than a few hours. By removing at least two feet, crews make sure they’re getting rid of all material that could harbor moisture or mold. This also lets them check and possibly remove wet insulation behind. While some minor Category 1 losses can be dried in place for under $2,900, anything with standing water or Category 2–3 contamination almost always warrants this approach to prevent lingering problems that would be far more costly later.
How much does water damage restoration cost in Cambria County? The cost of water damage restoration in Cambria County depends on the type of water, how far it spread, and how long it sat before drying started. For clean water events that are caught right away, typical extraction and drying cost $1,150–$7,200. Jobs involving deep or hidden moisture—like finished basements or old plaster walls—can cost more, especially if demolition is needed. If the water is contaminated or sewage is involved, cleanup runs $2,400–$11,500, accounting for hazardous material handling and extra safety precautions. The best way to avoid the higher end of those price bands is to act fast and document everything for your insurer.
Is it safe to stay in the house during drying? In most basic water extraction and drying projects (Category 1, clean water), it is possible to remain in your Cambria County home, provided you can isolate the affected area and tolerate noise and airflow from industrial equipment. However, for Category 2 or 3 events (gray or black water), or if there is confirmed mold growth, it’s safer to vacate during active cleanup. Airborne mold spores or bacteria can pose real health risks, especially to children, the elderly, or anyone with respiratory conditions. In these cases, professional crews set up containment and negative air pressure, but comfort and safety concerns usually make relocation prudent. For larger Category 3 jobs (costing $2,400–$11,500), insurers may cover “loss of use” for temporary housing if the property is uninhabitable.
