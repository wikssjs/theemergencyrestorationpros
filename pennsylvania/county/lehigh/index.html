What water damage restoration costs in Lehigh County

If you own a home in Lehigh County—especially in older Allentown neighborhoods—you might be dealing with unexpected water damage right now. This region’s restoration losses are shaped by homes that were built before 1950, especially row and twin homes around Center City, where aging plumbing and frequent pipe failures mean water issues happen more than most places. The typical Lehigh County house has a full basement, so water often finds its way into finished or unfinished below-grade spaces, driving up the cost and complexity of restoration.

Most restoration companies will give you an estimate only after seeing the loss, but you should have a clear idea of the expected range before anyone steps inside. Here are the most common services and their typical local price bands:

Water extraction + emergency drying (Cat 1) extraction, air movers, LGR dehumidifiers, daily moisture monitoring 3–5 days $1,250–$7,900

Choosing a restoration company in twenty minutes

In Lehigh County and throughout the dense Allentown–Bethlehem metro area, you don’t need to wait for a distant crew or settle for the only truck in range. With multiple established firms based locally, a reputable crew can typically be on-site within an hour. That said, during major rain events or heavy snow melts, demand spikes and availability tightens. No matter the rush, rushing your choice can cost you later.

If time is tight, focus on three things during the first phone call: (1) Confirm the company is actively registered as a Pennsylvania Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) for jobs over $500. (2) Ask if their workers are covered by current Pennsylvania workers’ compensation—don’t take a yes at face value, get the certificate. (3) Request that their lead technician carries a recognized restoration certification, such as IICRC, and will show it on arrival.

Before any equipment leaves the truck, have these in hand: a written scope of work, a copy of their HIC registration, and clear insurance details (liability and workers’ comp).

  • HIC registration number, verifiable online
  • Proof of general liability insurance in the company’s name
  • Workers’ compensation certificate (not a statement—real paperwork)
  • Lead technician’s restoration certification (IICRC card)
  • Written, line-item scope of work—not just “dry and repair as needed”
  • Three business-day right of cancellation form (required over $500)
  • No agreement to waive your insurance deductible (prohibited in PA)
  • A contract that spells out the price bands and payment terms
  • A start and finish estimate—don’t accept open-ended “as needed”
  • Contact number for urgent after-hours questions or complaints

Watch for these red flags: crews unloading gear before any paperwork is signed, blank-scope contracts (“price to be determined”), or being pressured to authorize work before you see details in writing. Don’t be rushed—legitimate firms will wait for your signature on an actual agreement.

What can be saved and what has to go

The mix of pre-1950 row and twin homes in Allentown and Lehigh County means most water losses hit a combination of plaster, solid hardwood, and full-basement materials. Fiberglass insulation and carpet pad? Those are almost always removed if they get wet, especially below grade. Drywall below the waterline is usually cut out in the affected zone whether or not the surface seems dry, due to quick mold risk. Plaster is common in older Lehigh County homes; it resists collapse but holds onto moisture, so drying takes longer and hidden pockets behind lath can harbor mold for weeks. Solid hardwood—typical in older houses—sometimes can be saved with aggressive floor-drying mats if caught quickly, but once it has cupped or blackened, removal is more likely than repair. Engineered wood and laminate rarely make it because their internal structure falls apart with water exposure.

Cabinets in Lehigh County homes are a wildcard; true hardwood boxes sometimes survive, but modern MDF or particleboard varieties usually do not if water has seeped under or behind. In basements—common here due to the typical full foundation—stud walls with fiberglass insulation and drywall go, while concrete and masonry can be cleaned and dried.

Beware of contractors pushing total demolition if only part of the material is affected. Likewise, a company saying “we can save everything” after blackwater or extended water exposure is setting you up for bigger (and pricier) mold remediation later.

Sewage backup: a different job entirely

Category 3 (sewage) water losses are not just smelly water problems. Lehigh County’s older neighborhoods, especially in Allentown, have long-standing sewer inflow issues during big storms—meaning backups are a recurring hazard, not a one-off accident. Sewage brings bacteria, viruses, and viruses into living space, so anyone entering must wear full PPE, and affected areas are immediately isolated and treated as biohazard zones.

The biggest difference is materials: all porous materials touched by sewage—carpet, padding, insulation, and most drywall—must be thrown out, even if they look okay. Disinfection, odor neutralization, and thorough cleaning follow removal. Involved areas are kept off-limits until finished. For health and legal reasons, no one should live in the affected area during cleanup. Restoration is stricter and pricier: expect $2,600–$12,500 for a complete cleanup, including disposal, disinfection, and air scrubbing.

Long-term, backflow prevention valves can reduce repeat events, but check with your township; municipal cost-share programs in Lehigh County are not the norm. Without protection, any heavy rain can mean a repeat claim. If a contractor isn’t suiting up or wants to “just dry it out,” move on—they’re risking your health and a denied insurance claim.

Immediate steps for a Lehigh County homeowner with active water

If you’re standing in water, do these in order. First, turn off your home’s water supply at the main shut-off. Next, cut power to any circuits serving wet areas—only if your panel is dry and safely accessible. Never wade to a breaker. Before touching anything else, photograph every room, wall, and damaged pile—insurance adjusters want to see original conditions.

Move what you can: pick up rugs, electronics, papers, or furniture, and place them on dry ground or plastic crates. Skip using a household vacuum for standing water; these aren’t rated for water and can be a shock hazard. Leave the heavy extraction to the professionals.

Call your insurance company before, or at the same time as, reaching a restoration contractor. Both need photographic proof of the loss, so save every image. Don’t worry about the order as much as the documentation.

The most common and costly mistake in Lehigh County? Waiting until morning. Mold can start in as little as 24–36 hours, and the price difference between same-day drying and later mold remediation can be several thousand dollars—going from $1,250 to $6,300 just for delayed action. Moving fast isn’t panic; it’s economics.

Credentials and permits for restoration work in Lehigh County

Pennsylvania does not have a state license for water damage restoration or mold remediation—anyone can print business cards, so don’t let impressive branding fool you. The professional standard is IICRC certification; always ask to see the lead technician’s card on site. There is no Lehigh County or statewide permit required for standard water extraction and drying, but if structural repairs (like drywall or framing replacement) are needed after drying, most local municipalities—including Allentown—require a building permit for those repairs. In South Whitehall Township and similar areas, basic water mitigation without rebuilding is not usually permitted.

Verify the contractor’s HIC (Home Improvement Contractor) registration at hic.attorneygeneral.gov for any job over $500, and double-check workers’ compensation coverage at wcais.pa.gov—never accept just verbal assurance. Insurance certificates should be in writing and name the company you’re hiring, not an unrelated parent or subcontractor.

Before any extractors or fans are plugged in, get a signed contract with a three-day cancellation notice as required by state law, a written work scope, and full insurance details. If they can’t provide these, find someone who can.

How a Lehigh County crew dries a house

The real work starts with moisture mapping: a skilled crew measures and logs moisture readings (walls, floors, framing) using professional meters, not just pinching drywall with their fingers. These daily readings get charted to track progress toward a drying goal—a moisture content matching unaffected areas or manufacturer specs. This log matters; it protects you and proves to your insurer the house is actually dry.

Crews bring air movers (for airflow) and LGR (low-grain refrigerant) dehumidifiers to handle both surface evaporation and the region’s persistent humidity. Lehigh County sees over 6,100 heating degree days and heavy snow melt, so outdoor conditions rarely help with drying—closing windows and keeping equipment running 24/7 is standard procedure, even overnight. Equipment noise is part of the process; don’t unplug it, or you reset the drying clock.

Competent firms perform daily site visits for meter readings and log updates. Ask for copies—the logs should show falling moisture numbers day by day. If a crew can’t hand over these daily logs, they weren’t measuring, and you have no proof the house is dry.

Drying is done when all target readings are hit and documented. Don’t accept “it feels dry to the touch” as a finishing test; in Lehigh County’s climate, that’s how mold claims start after the crew leaves.

Finished basements and water damage in Lehigh County

In Lehigh County’s pre-1950 homes and newer developments alike, full basements are the norm. When water enters below grade, it acts differently from upstairs—hydrostatic pressure can force water through cracks, and without a floor drain, water may sit and wick into walls, hidden framing, and insulation for hours before you notice. Finished basements double the remediation burden: what would be a one-day extraction in an unfinished space now means cutting drywall, pulling soaked insulation, and drying carpet and pad (if salvageable), all hidden by finished walls.

Sump pump failure is the leading single cause of basement water claims here, especially during spring thaws when Lehigh County’s 36-inch frost depth finally gives way and ground saturation spikes. Battery backup systems are an investment—cheap compared to the $1,250–$7,900 for full extraction and drying if a power outage hits during a storm.

Always check behind finished walls and under raised floors. Lehigh Valley humidity means “dry to the touch” isn’t enough; moisture meters are the best way to avoid a hidden mold bloom a month later.

Frequently asked questions — water damage restoration in Lehigh County

Is it safe to stay in the house during drying?

In most Lehigh County water damage cases, it’s safe to stay in undamaged areas of the house during drying—provided the affected areas are isolated and you can avoid the work zone. Modern drying equipment is noisy, and for larger jobs, the increased heat and humidity may make some spaces uncomfortable. For Category 1 (clean water) losses, temporary relocation isn’t usually needed. However, for Category 3 (sewage) jobs—common in parts of Allentown with sewer backup risk—you should leave the affected zones entirely and sometimes vacate the home until after cleanup. If mold remediation is necessary, expect additional containment and air filtration, which can restrict use of much of the space. These safety measures are best discussed onsite with your contractor, especially for jobs over $4,100.

Is bleach effective against mold?

Bleach seems like a go-to, but in Lehigh County’s mostly older, plaster and drywall homes, bleach only removes surface mold stains and doesn’t penetrate porous building materials. Mold roots (hyphae) stay behind, especially in drywall, plaster, and wood framing, and regrowth is likely once surfaces are just “bleached.” Professional mold remediation always involves physically removing contaminated materials, HEPA vacuuming, and using EPA-registered antimicrobials—not household bleach—plus a clearance test. That process, for Lehigh County homes, runs $1,600–$11,000, much more than DIY supplies, but it actually fixes the problem instead of hiding it.

Is emergency after-hours service more expensive in Lehigh County?

Most local restoration firms in Lehigh County, given the dense regional market, do not charge premium “emergency” surcharges for true water emergencies if insurance is involved, since pricing is often dictated by national carrier rate sheets. Private-pay jobs may see higher minimum charges after hours, but the bigger variable is how much work is needed rather than when. A weekend callout for water extraction and drying can run the same as a daytime one—$1,250–$7,900—unless special trades (like electricians) are required outside normal hours. Ask up front for full pricing and watch for hidden fees in your contract.

How do I know the house is actually dry?

Lehigh County restoration professionals should provide daily meter readings for all affected building materials, logged and tracked against a target drying goal—a numerical moisture content, not just “it feels dry.” Ask for these logs before work is completed. The absence of documented readings is a red flag, especially in this region’s humid climate and full-basement homes. Visual checks are not enough, as framed and insulated assemblies in older homes can harbor hidden moisture. Proper monitoring reduces your risk of a later mold claim (potentially $1,600–$11,000 if missed).

Is sewer backup a known problem in Lehigh County?

Absolutely. Allentown and several older Lehigh County neighborhoods have documented issues with sanitary and storm sewer inflow, especially during heavy rain or rapid snowmelt. Backups are a regular claim, not a fluke. This means even a properly maintained home can face a sudden Category 3 (sewage) event, requiring full biohazard protocol at a cost of $2,600–$12,500. If you’re in a flood- or backup-prone area, consider backflow prevention valves, though most municipalities in this county do not have cost-share programs for them.

What is the difference between water damage class and category?

In Lehigh County, as anywhere, “class” refers to how much and what parts of your house are wet—the volume and what materials are affected. For example, Class 1 may mean only part of one room’s floor is wet; Class 3 often means ceilings, insulation, and walls are saturated. “Category” describes the cleanliness of the water: Category 1 is clean water (from supply lines), while Category 3 is grossly contaminated (as with sewage backups, a known local risk). Category determines what gets cleaned versus tossed and dramatically changes your bill—Category 1 extraction is $1,250–$7,900, but sewage work is much higher.

Is basement flooding covered by insurance in Pennsylvania?

Basement water damage in Lehigh County is only covered by standard homeowners insurance if the cause is sudden and accidental—like a burst pipe or failed washing machine. Water entering through groundwater, foundation leaks, or overland flooding (water coming from outside) is almost never covered unless you carry a separate flood insurance policy. With finished basements so common in local housing stock, even a small claim can escalate to $3,200–$7,900 for full drying and repairs—not something you want out of pocket. Always check your specific policy and consider flood insurance if you’re near the Lehigh River or Little Lehigh Creek.

What does structural drying cost if I extracted the water myself?

If you were able to handle initial water extraction using a pump or shop vac, and only need professional drying to standard (with air movers and dehumidifiers), the typical Lehigh County cost is lower. Structural drying only, post-extraction, runs $1,050–$7,400 as long as the area is quickly addressed and materials are not too heavily saturated. Be sure a professional crew does moisture mapping—DIY fans and dehumidifiers rarely speed drying as fast as it needs to move in an older, wood-framed Lehigh Valley home.

What is IICRC certification and does it matter?

IICRC stands for the Institute of Inspection, Cleaning and Restoration Certification—a nonprofit standard-setter in the restoration industry. In Pennsylvania (including Lehigh County), there is no required state license for water or mold work, so IICRC credentials on your technician’s card are the best independent proof of real training. The S500 (water damage) and S520 (mold) standards are what most insurers expect to see referenced on your job’s paperwork. Without them, your insurer could dispute the work or pricing. For a job costing $4,100–$6,300, insist the crew is IICRC certified and ask to see the card on arrival.

Do I need separate flood insurance in Lehigh County?

Yes, if your house might ever suffer water coming “from outside”—such as the Lehigh River, Little Lehigh Creek, or even surface runoff—regular homeowners insurance in Lehigh County does not cover those losses. A separate flood insurance policy is needed for any overland water entry or groundwater flooding. With much of the county’s housing near flood-prone corridors and with finished basements in many homes, a single flood can result in losses between $3,200–$11,000. Check your risk on FEMA maps and talk to your insurer; premiums depend on your floodplain location and elevation.

What equipment should a restoration crew bring?

Expect a legitimate Lehigh County restoration crew to bring commercial water extractors, a mix of air movers and LGR dehumidifiers, moisture meters (both pin and infrared), containment materials (for large or Category 3 jobs), and HEPA filtration units for mold or sewage work. For finished basements, they may also have injectidry systems or floor drying mats. Large losses may require portable generators if house power is out. Every reputable crew should have these on hand—especially for jobs running $1,050–$12,500—or they are not properly prepared for all conditions in our local housing stock.
