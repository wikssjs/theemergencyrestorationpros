What to do right now

If you haven’t called anyone yet, here’s what actually matters—in order. First, shut off the water at the main. In most Perkasie homes, the shutoff will be in the basement, which is common with full-basement construction. If any electrical circuits touch wet areas and the panel is dry and safely accessible, shut them off. If you must walk through water or the panel is in a flooded area, wait for a professional—it’s not worth the risk.

Before moving a single thing, grab your phone and photograph every angle—damage, standing water, items on the floor—everything. This is evidence for your insurer. After that, move any contents you can up onto dry flooring or even out of the area, especially cardboard boxes and soft furniture. Never use a household vacuum on standing water—it’s a fire and shock hazard.

Call your insurer immediately or at the same time you call a restoration contractor. The order isn’t critical as long as you have those photos. Many homeowners in Perkasie delay until morning—this is the mistake that most increases cost. Mold can start forming within 24–48 hours. Putting the call off until the next weekday morning can turn a routine dry-out ($1,300–$8,100) into multi-thousand-dollar mold remediation ($1,600–$11,500).

Category and class: the two words that set your price

Two terms decide your water restoration bill in Perkasie: category and class—neither means size. Category covers how contaminated the water is; think of it as a cleanliness scale from 1 to 3. Class tells how much of your structure soaked it up—small patch in a closet, or the whole finished basement saturated from floor to ceiling? Most misunderstand and think “big” automatically means “worse.” It doesn’t.

It’s possible for a small Category 3 (sewage or long-standing water) event confined to one room to run higher than a whole-basement clean water event. For example, a limited sewer backup can easily top $2,700–$13,000, compared to a typical clean-water flood. Category changes with time: clean water from your supply line left sitting more than 48–72 hours can become contaminated as it contacts materials and goes stagnant. So, waiting turns a cheaper Category 1 into a Category 2 or 3, with costs rising accordingly.

What a Pennsylvania restoration contractor must actually hold

Pennsylvania does not license restoration or mold remediation contractors. IICRC certification is the industry standard here—it’s voluntary but what most insurers (and sensible homeowners) expect. Always ask to see your lead technician’s IICRC card and verify the certifying organization’s name. For mold work, IICRC S520 is the reference document used by insurance.

Permits in Bucks County are usually required if restoration work involves reconstructing framing or structural elements—emergency drying, water extraction, and non-structural work typically do not need a permit, but local enforcement varies. In most Perkasie scenarios, the code authority will clarify this if framing is affected. Always ask up front if a permit application is being filed for your project.

Home improvement contractors here must be registered for work over $500. Ask for their Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration and check it at the official state site. Confirm proof of workers’ compensation—use the WCAIS portal to double-check active coverage. Make sure you get a written contract with costs and scope before any crew sets up equipment; that’s your protection under Pennsylvania’s Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act.

What can be saved and what has to go

The truth in lower Bucks, including Perkasie, is that restoration after water damage is usually about triage, not “saving everything.” Fiberglass insulation and carpet pads are rarely salvageable once soaked, especially after plumbing failures that are common in our aging housing stock. Standard drywall below the waterline gets cut out almost every time—it’s faster, cleaner, and reduces mold risk. Solid hardwood can sometimes be saved with specialty floor drying if you catch it early. Engineered hardwood and laminate? Usually a loss, because swollen edges and delamination are common.

Older homes in Perkasie built pre-1980 often have plaster walls rather than drywall. Plaster absorbs moisture slower but dries out much less readily—that means longer drying times and a higher risk of hidden mold if rushed. Cabinets are case-by-case. Plywood boxes can sometimes be dried and sanitized, but particleboard cabinets, especially in finished basements, usually don’t make it if water sat even half a day.

Be wary of any contractor who suggests “tearing out everything” automatically or, just as badly, promises “no demolition at all.” Full demolition is overkill unless you’re dealing with a massive Category 3 event, and promising to save obviously saturated materials only sets up a mold claim later. The right contractor identifies and documents what’s truly unsalvageable.

Documenting and recovering contents in Perkasie

Contents—your personal property—are usually the most under-claimed items in a Perkasie water loss. Full pack-out means the crew inventories, photographs, and moves affected items off-site for cleaning or storage while structural drying happens. Every item should be documented (make, model, condition, serial number where relevant). Photograph everything before moving, especially in basements or any finished space where dampness is widespread.

Your policy handles contents (furniture, electronics, clothing, tools) and structure (walls, flooring, cabinets) separately. Don’t rely on memory—upholstered furniture and mattresses exposed to Category 3 water (such as sewage backups from regional sanitary issues) and most particleboard furniture are too risky to restore. Solid wood furniture, electronics not directly soaked, and items with hard surfaces are the best candidates for salvage.

When hiring a company, make sure they specify in writing which contents they’ll remove and attempt to restore—and which will be listed for replacement on your claim. Precision in this step saves money and months of frustration with insurance.

Outside water, inside water

In Bucks County, it matters whether the water entered from inside—like a burst supply line—or from outside, such as flooding from the Delaware River, Neshaminy Creek, or Pennypack Creek. Many Perkasie homeowners find too late that their standard policy excludes rising floodwater, even though homes look identical after cleanup.

Flood insurance is separate and must be purchased well before you need it (there’s typically a 30-day waiting period). Being in a mapped flood zone—often designated by FEMA based on local creek boundaries—can make this insurance more expensive, but even out-of-zone properties experience overbank flooding during storm remnants. Coverage for a burst indoor pipe (handled under regular homeowner’s) is not the same as “flood”—even with identical repair bills.

Bottom line: a flood adjuster will deny coverage for outside water that reached your house from rising natural sources, no matter what structural or contents items are affected. If you live near known flood sources, ask your insurer for written specifics.
