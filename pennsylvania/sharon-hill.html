Regulation, certification and verification in Sharon Hill

Pennsylvania does not license restoration or mold remediation firms, so a company’s business card isn’t a guarantee of expertise in Sharon Hill or anywhere in Delaware County. Restoration companies are only regulated under general contracting rules. The industry uses the IICRC S500 and S520 standards for water and mold work, but this is voluntary. Ask if the lead technician on your job holds IICRC certification and request to see their card—it means they’ve passed actual exams specific to water damage or mold mitigation.

Permits in Sharon Hill are typically required only if your project involves reconstruction, not mitigation (like carpet removal and drying). Be aware that in places like Upper Darby, interior demolition that includes drywall removal after water damage often triggers a permit. For small leaks that require only drying and no major removal—especially in nearby Springfield Township—permits are usually not needed.

Always verify that your contractor carries current Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration for jobs over $500. In addition, all employers must have workers’ compensation insurance—double-check coverage by searching the company at WCAIS. Before crews start extraction, get a clear written contract outlining scope, timeline, price, and payment terms. Pennsylvania law requires written contracts for work over $500 and gives you three business days to cancel.

How a Sharon Hill crew dries a house

In Sharon Hill, an experienced crew starts with moisture mapping—using meters and infrared tools to check not just obvious wet spots, but walls, baseplates, and floors you can’t see or feel. They’ll document every reading, creating a moisture log. This isn’t bureaucracy: the daily log protects you and the contractor, shows your insurance how much got wet, and guides equipment placement.

Expect a combination of air movers (which accelerate evaporation by blowing air across surfaces) and LGR (low-grain refrigerant) dehumidifiers, which can handle our muggy summer air and draw out what rises as vapor. Skimping on dehumidifiers makes drying fail, especially with southeastern PA’s humid climate (over 1,450 cooling degree days per year). Equipment will seem loud and relentless, but don’t shut it off at night—the process runs continuously, and every interruption adds drying time and potential for mold.

Each day, the crew should stop by, take new readings with their meter, and mark progress toward a drying goal—a scientifically established moisture content for materials, not a guess. Before paying, ask to see these logs; if they don't have them, you can't prove it’s dry. A diligent crew will show you every single reading, start to finish.

Sewage backup: a different job entirely

Cleaning up sewage in Sharon Hill calls for far more than just mop-up—a combined sewer overflow in Chester City is a known hazard, and these backups often bring more than unpleasant odor. Proper crews suit up in full PPE, set up containment barriers, and immediately remove porous materials like carpet, padding, and some drywall, regardless of how clean they look; disinfecting is not enough for these items. Every contact surface is disinfected and deodorized with professional products. Occupants shouldn’t be inside while work is active, both for safety and to avoid tracking pathogens.

Combined sewers make this a recurring risk in parts of Delaware County—not a random accident—so prevention options include installing a backflow valve (installed on your main sewer line to stop water from reversing) if the city permits them. Municipalities here do not typically cost-share for this work, so all costs fall to homeowners. Full sewage backup cleanup jobs in Sharon Hill usually run between $2,400–$11,500 depending on scope and materials affected.

After the repair: hardening the house

Once the immediate mess is dealt with, some preventive measures are worth every dollar, while others are rarely justified in Sharon Hill homes. First up: automatic water shutoff valves that sense leaks and shut your supply (common cost: $350–$900 installed), which are well worth it if you travel or have a history of leaks. Next, swap out rubber supply lines on washers and water heaters for braided stainless ones—this costs under $150, and makes sense for aging plumbing in the area’s older homes.

Adding a sump pump with battery backup ($500–$1,800) pays back in full if your basement is finished or prone to groundwater issues. Backflow valves are recommended only for homes hooked into combined sewers, mostly in Chester City, and cost about $900–$2,800. As for water heater drain pans and wall-mounted leak sensors ($20–$100 apiece), these are low-cost peace of mind for finished spaces. Full-house water alarms and expensive monitoring systems aren’t needed in most Sharon Hill houses—stick to the cost-effective basics given our 30-inch frost depth and relatively moderate freeze risk.

How long the whole thing takes

Water damage restoration in Sharon Hill splits into two phases: mitigation and reconstruction. Mitigation—think extraction, drying, mold treatment—typically takes between 3 to 7 days and is handled by crews trained to move fast, especially since many local firms offer 24/7 service. This urgent first phase is where most of your out-of-pocket money (and insurance dollars) will go, and it’s usually direct-billed.

Once the house is dry and equipment leaves, many homeowners are surprised by a gap—often several days to weeks—before reconstruction starts. This is normal, not abandonment. Reconstruction involves new drywall, flooring, and other repairs, and must be permitted and scheduled, especially where code enforcement is active (as in Upper Darby). In the dense Delaware County market, the sheer number of competing contractors can lead to longer lead times for reconstruction as firms juggle multiple jobs.

So, be aware: mitigation is quick (days), the post-drying lull is expected, and reconstruction is the slowest and most schedule-bound piece. Clarify upfront if you’ll be using the same company for both phases or if you’ll need to contract your own trades.

Filing a water damage claim in Pennsylvania

The biggest insurance mistake Sharon Hill homeowners make is not knowing what’s covered. Sudden internal water events—like a burst pipe—are generally covered under standard homeowners policies. But any flooding that originates outside the home, such as water coming up from the Delaware River or Chester Creek, is excluded unless you carry a separate flood insurance policy. Many only find this out when the claim gets denied.

Maintenance neglect and long-standing leaks are also usually excluded, and a typical policy will not pay for mold caused by neglect or activation of gradual seepage. Sewer and drain backups are not a built-in part of most policies here; they require a specific rider, which is highly recommended in areas with combined sewer issues like Chester City. Watch for whether your policy pays “actual cash value” (depreciated based on the age of materials) or “replacement cost,” which covers the full new cost. This can be a difference of thousands in a claim’s payout.

After a loss, the restoration crew or your adjuster will generate a “scope of loss” detailing the damage and repair plan—read it and ask questions. Direct billing and assignment of benefits let contractors bill your insurer directly, giving up some control over payment timing but sparing you paperwork headaches. Under the Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act, you’re entitled to a written contract for work over $500 and have three business days to cancel a contract. Be aware it is illegal here for a contractor to offer to absorb or waive your insurance deductible—if someone does, it’s a code red for fraud and may void your coverage.

Is a shop vacuum enough for standing water?

What does structural drying cost if I extracted the water myself?
