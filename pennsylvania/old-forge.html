Salvage decisions after a water loss

What’s worth saving and what isn’t after a water loss in Old Forge comes down to materials, water type, and the time it sat. With homes often dating to the early 20th century, plaster and lath walls and finished basements dominate, making hidden moisture a common headache here—much more so than in newer neighborhoods. Plaster holds moisture far longer than drywall, increasing the risk of mold behind walls and in old basement assemblies. In most cases, plaster can’t just be left to “dry out” if it has stayed wet for days; it needs monitoring, and often small holes drilled for meter readings and air movement. But immediate demolition isn’t always needed—unless mold is already visible or conditions have been wet for 72+ hours.

Fiberglass insulation and carpet pad, once saturated, usually need removal. Structural framing, if promptly dried, is often salvageable. Solid hardwood floors can sometimes be saved with specialty drying mats, but that’s rare unless action is taken within the first 24–48 hours. Engineered wood (common in some remodeled Old Forge homes) swells and delaminates rapidly, which usually means removal. Drywall below the waterline almost always gets cut – two feet or so above visible damage – but above that, it can sometimes be dried in place.

Finished basements unique to Old Forge often hide moisture beneath paneling, tile, or behind bar areas—so perimeter probing and a cautious approach are warranted. Cabinets depend on substrate: plywood boxes fare far better than particle board when wet, though neither holds up if sitting in water for long. If a contractor says every single surface must go on day one, that’s overkill and drives costs up. Conversely, anyone who promises all finishes can always be saved is setting up a mold problem for later. The best answer: selective demo, meter readings, and a willingness to change course if conditions don’t improve fast.

What proper drying actually looks like

Professional drying in Old Forge is all about deliberate, documented process—not just fans blowing and equipment noise. It starts with moisture mapping: using meters to log exactly where materials are wet or dry, including behind plaster and finished basement assemblies commonly found here. Crews should make a meter reading log at the start, not just visually guess. Air movers circulate air across surfaces while LGR (low-grain refrigerant) dehumidifiers pull moisture from the air—both run continuously, often for 3–5 days, and turning them off at night ruins progress. The drying goal isn’t a guess; it’s a specified moisture content based on readings from non-affected areas of your home or known material standards.

Daily monitoring visits are non-negotiable. Each day, techs should record meter readings in a log—if they can’t show you those, assume measurements aren’t happening. Plaster walls, common in Old Forge, may need extra time or drilled access for probes, especially in winter when this region’s outside air is dry but cold, raising heating costs but speeding up the process. In summer, with only 850 cooling degree days, you won’t get the same “free” dehumidification from air conditioning, so proper equipment sizing matters even more.

Before accepting that a house is dry, ask to see the documented moisture logs. If you get a shrug or “trust me, it’s all good,” call that the red flag it is. Proper drying is measured, not guessed, and involves written records you can keep.

The first hour: what to do before anyone arrives

If you’ve just discovered flooding in your Old Forge home—and the restoration company hasn’t arrived—here’s your sequence, in order:

  • Shut off the main water supply if the leak is fresh or ongoing. Your shutoff is often in the basement; don’t wade through deep water to reach it if unsafe.
  • Kill power to affected circuits only if the electrical panel is dry, accessible, and you do not need to stand in water to do so.
  • Photograph every area, surface, and damaged item before moving anything. Images matter most if insurance is involved; snap before/after shots from each angle.
  • Move contents (furniture, boxes, electronics) up onto dry surfaces, not just to another wet corner—wood blocks or counters are best.
  • Avoid plugging in or using any household vacuum to remove water—ordinary units are not rated for this and can cause shock or become biohazards.

Call your insurer as soon as you know there’s damage. Whether you do this before or after the restoration company makes less difference than you think, but claims are smoother with your own photos and documentation taken at the first moment. What costs far more: waiting until morning. Mold can start within 24–48 hours, and delays often mean you move from the $1,150–$7,300 range for emergency drying to full-blown mold remediation. Don’t give mold the head start—every hour counts, especially in basements common to Old Forge.

What a Pennsylvania restoration contractor must actually hold

Pennsylvania does not license restoration or mold contractors at the state level. This means that a business card, website, or a local phone number in itself tells you little about a company’s qualifications to handle water or mold jobs specific to Old Forge. Insurers expect contractors to follow IICRC S500 standards for water work and IICRC S520 for mold, so always ask if your lead technician can show actual IICRC certification—most carry a wallet card.

For contracts above $500, written Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration from the Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General is mandatory. Before signing, verify their HIC number online. Scranton and surrounding municipalities require building permits only when work moves from emergency drying to structural repair—replacing drywall, framing, or flooring. Non-structural, minor finish work after mitigation is typically permit-exempt.

Ask for a certificate of insurance naming your address as certificate holder, and verify active workers’ compensation coverage for each person on site. This can be checked through the state system. No proper company will hesitate. Demand the signed written contract up front—Pennsylvania’s Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act (HICPA) requires it, and you always get a 3-day right of cancellation. Do not allow work to begin until you have these protections in writing.
