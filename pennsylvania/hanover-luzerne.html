How a Hanover crew dries a house

Drying out a home in Hanover after water damage is a systematic process, not a matter of just running fans. Local professionals start by mapping and documenting moisture throughout the affected structure—using pin or pinless moisture meters, they log readings from the floor, walls, and framing each day. These logs create a permanent record, tracking progress and showing your insurer that the work is legitimate. Air movers are set out to push damp air off surfaces, while LGR (Low Grain Refrigerant) dehumidifiers pull moisture from the air and help bring the home’s humidity down to safe levels. Don’t expect the equipment to be whisper-quiet, but resist the urge to turn it off at night—thousands of gallons can pass through a space in just days, and shutting a dehumidifier down adds unnecessary time (and mold risk). Each day, the technician checks progress. They aren't guessing when to stop; there's a "drying goal," meaning a target moisture content that's typically the same as unaffected areas. The industry standard locally (IICRC S500) expects written, dated logs and a photo record—if a crew can’t produce these, they probably weren’t checking. Hanover’s outdoor climate—cooler temperatures, high humidity, and long heating seasons—affects how fast a structure dries, since ambient air in northeastern Pennsylvania isn't always ideal for drying. Expect a few days with heavy equipment running non-stop in older, tight basements common in Wilkes-Barre and Kingston. Your best quality check: simply ask for the daily moisture content log. If the company stalls or can't show you detailed readings, look elsewhere.

How restorers classify water damage

Understanding damage in your Hanover home comes down to two separate scales—CATEGORY (what’s in the water) and CLASS (how far it went). CATEGORY describes contamination. Category 1 is clean water—think a supply line or rain. Category 2, “grey,” may carry soaps or dirt, like a washer overflow. Category 3 is “black,” which includes sewage, storm water, or anything left standing 72 hours or more. This matters, because a tiny Category 3 loss (say, a couple square feet of sewer backup) can cost more than a much larger clean-water event—easy for a scope to hit $2,400–$11,500 even on a small footprint. CLASS covers how much the structure has absorbed. Class 1 is just damp carpets; by Class 4, you’re dealing with deep saturation in hardwood, plaster, or concrete, which is common in the old basements of Wilkes-Barre and Kingston. Time works against you. Clean water quickly turns grey or black; every hour it sits, the risk, complexity, and cost rise. That’s why quick extraction after a pipe burst is much cheaper than waiting a day with a flooded, unpowered basement.

What insurance covers — and the exclusion that surprises people

Flood coverage is a separate policy entirely, and you can’t buy it after the river crests. Gradual damage, like dripping supply lines or foundation seepage over time, is also typically excluded, as insurers expect you to maintain the home—same for new mold growth due to postponed drying. Not all policies include coverage for sewer backups by default; it’s almost always a rider. In Wilkes-Barre, unprotected basements near the levee system regularly see claims denied unless this extra coverage was bought up front. Payouts come as either “actual cash value” (depreciated for age) or “replacement cost” (new, less your deductible). Many firms in Luzerne County can direct-bill your carrier or may ask for an assignment of benefits, meaning they deal directly with the insurance company for payment. If a contractor offers to “cover your deductible,” walk away. It’s both a warning of potential fraud and a common reason insurers deny payment.
